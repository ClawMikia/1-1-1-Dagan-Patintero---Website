What is Patintero?

Also known across the archipelago as:

TubiganIring-iringSilatongLawin-lawin

Patintero is a Filipino street game usually played by two teams on a rectangular court scratched into the pavement or drawn with tsinelas heels and chalk. One team — the taya — guards the lines that divide the court; the other team must run the entire length of the court and back without ever being touched by a guard while standing on their line.

Where the name comes from

"Patintero" borrows from the Spanish word tinta (ink), a nod to the days when the court's lines were literally inked or chalked onto the street before every match. In many provinces it's simply called Tubigan, after the water once used to mark the lines instead.

Why it endures

For generations of Filipino children, patintero has been an after-school ritual — no equipment needed beyond open pavement, quick feet, and teammates willing to call out a warning. It sharpens footwork, spatial awareness, and split-second decision making, and it's still played in barangay streets, school intramurals, and even sipped into Palarong Pambansa as a folk sport today.

📜 How to Play

The Court

The yard is a grid you configure yourself — pick Munti, Katamtaman, or Malaki for the size. Your home lines are the very top and very bottom rows — guards can never stand there. Between them run your chosen number of guarded chalk lines, split across horizontal patrol lines with one always locked to the straight-down-the-middle line.

Your Move

You're the runner. Move one step at a time with WASD or the ↑↓←→ keys — or the on-screen pad on touch devices.

The Guards

Each CPU guard is bound to their own line and can't leave it — three patrol left-right along their horizontal line, and the fourth patrols up-down along the center line. They react to where you are, so timing your dash matters more than speed alone.

Tags & Crossings

  • Land on the exact same tile as a guard and you're tagged — three tags and the round is over.
  • Reach the far home line and it counts as one crossing. Then turn around and head back — that's your next crossing.
  • Complete 3 crossings before the timer hits zero to win the round.

Why It's Special

Real patintero is a game of nerve — waiting for a guard to drift just far enough, then bursting through the gap. This digital yard keeps that same rhythm: watch the lines, read the guards, and go.

🇵🇭 About Patintero

Patintero is one of the most recognizable Laro ng Lahi (games of the people) in the Philippines — a street sport that needs nothing more than open pavement and a few willing players.

Traditional Setup

A rectangular court is drawn on the ground, split into equal boxes by a set of horizontal lines and one line running down the middle. Traditionally the lines were scratched with a stick, chalked in, or wet down with water — hence one of its old names, Tubigan ("watered").

Two Teams

One team are the taya (guards), each assigned to defend one line and forbidden from stepping off it. The other team are the runners, who must cross the whole court from one end to the other and back without a guard's hand ever touching them while they're on a line.

Scoring

A runner who makes it all the way across and back scores a point for their team. Get tagged, and it's the guards' turn to run — teams swap roles, and the game continues until an agreed score or time limit is reached.

A Living Tradition

Patintero has been played in Filipino neighborhoods for generations, passed from older kids to younger ones with no rulebook needed — just a demonstration and a dare. It still turns up in barangay fiestas, PE classes, and folk-games competitions across the country today.
